Employee vs. Contractor Classification for Startups

Calling someone a contractor doesn't make them one. Misclassification is one of the more expensive mistakes an early startup can make—and one of the easiest to avoid with the right paperwork.

Published August 7, 2026 by Alex Ravski

Why classification isn't just a label

Whether someone is an employee or an independent contractor is a legal conclusion based on how the relationship actually works, not what the contract calls it or what either side prefers for tax reasons. Regulators—the IRS, state labor agencies, the Department of Labor—look at the substance: who controls the work, the schedule, the tools, and whether the person is economically dependent on your company.

What regulators actually look at

  • Behavioral control: Do you set their hours, dictate how the work gets done, require specific tools or processes?
  • Financial control: Are they paid a flat fee for deliverables, or a regular wage regardless of output? Do they invest in their own equipment and take on their own business risk?
  • Relationship type: Is the work core to what your company does, or incidental? Is the engagement open-ended or tied to a defined project?
  • State-specific tests: California runs the toughest independent contractor standard in the country, the so-called "ABC test," and defaults to treating everyone as an employee unless you can clear all three letters. Roughly: nobody's telling this person how to do their job day to day, what they're doing has nothing to do with what your company actually sells, and they've already got their own business doing this same kind of work for other clients too. Letter B is the one that catches founders off guard—a plumber fixing a leak in your office is obviously outside your usual business; an engineer building your product is a much harder sell as a "contractor" no matter what the invoice says.

How to actually protect contractor status

If you've concluded someone genuinely is a contractor, how you manage the relationship day to day is what protects that classification—not just the agreement they signed. In practice: don't set their hours or supervise the work closely, don't hand them company equipment unless the work requires it, don't invite them to all-hands meetings or give them a company title or staff-style email signature, pay them against invoices rather than a regular salary-style paycheck, and don't let a single project quietly turn into open-ended, full-time-equivalent work without revisiting whether the classification still holds.

What misclassification actually costs

If a "contractor" is later found to be an employee, the company can owe back payroll taxes, unpaid overtime, unemployment insurance contributions, and penalties—often retroactive to when the relationship started. It also surfaces in diligence: acquirers and investors ask about worker classification specifically because it's a common source of hidden liability.

The IP trap nobody expects

Classification isn't only a tax and labor issue. Under default law, a contractor generally owns the IP they create unless a written agreement assigns it to you—the opposite default from employees in most states. A startup that treats someone as a contractor without a signed IP assignment can end up not owning code, designs, or content that person built for the company.

FAQ

Can I convert a contractor to an employee later?

Yes, and it's common as a company matures. The transition should be documented with a new offer letter and, if needed, updated IP assignment terms.

Does a written contractor agreement guarantee contractor status?

No. A written agreement helps, but if the actual working relationship looks like employment, the agreement's label won't control the outcome with regulators.
